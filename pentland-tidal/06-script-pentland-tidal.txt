There is a power station at the top of Scotland that almost nobody has ever seen. Ferries sail straight over it every single day, and not one passenger knows it is there. It has no chimney, no tower and no fuel, and it can do something no wind farm in this country can do.

At a quarter to three on a grey afternoon, a small car ferry heads out across the Pentland Firth. On the open deck, a lorry driver from Thurso drinks his tea and looks down at the sea. The water is moving faster than the boat. White foam races past the side, small whirlpools open and close, and the ferry has to point sideways just to stay on course. More than 30 metres below him, on the seabed, a giant underwater turbine is spinning in the dark. Its blades are nearly as wide as a cricket pitch is long.

This is a stretch of sea so dangerous that old sailors gave its currents names. Nearby is an island where everyone packed up and left in 1962. Across the water is a test site in Orkney that refused to give up. And somewhere far from the sea is a brass machine built in 1872. We will come back to that machine at the very end. Keep one number in mind as we go. It is not a speed and it is not a price. It is a length of time.

To understand why that number matters, you need to know how many times Britain has invented a way to get power from the sea, and then let it go. In 1974, an engineer in Edinburgh called Stephen Salter designed a machine made of floats that bobbed up and down with the waves, like ducks on a pond. That bobbing movement was turned into electricity. People called it Salter's Duck. For a few years, Britain was the world leader in wave power. Then, in the early 1980s, the government cut the funding, and the money went elsewhere.

That was the first loss. The second was on land. In 1987, on a hill in Orkney called Burgar Hill, a group of British companies built a huge wind turbine. It was one of the most powerful in the world at the time. But the orders never came, and the wind turbine industry moved across the North Sea to Denmark. Today, Britain has one of the biggest offshore wind farms in the world, but most of the turbines in it were designed in other countries.

Then it happened again, this time in the water. In April 2008, in Strangford Lough in Northern Ireland, a Bristol company called Marine Current Turbines put a machine called SeaGen in the sea. It was the first full-size tidal turbine in the world selling power to the grid. Four years later, the company was sold to the German firm Siemens. In November 2014, Pelamis Wave Power of Edinburgh, whose red sea snake machine had powered the world's first wave farm, went bust. A few months later, Aquamarine Power in Orkney went bust too.

Britain never ran out of good ideas. It ran out of patience.

That is why the next part is so important. Because one place never gave up. In 2003, in the town of Stromness in Orkney, a testing centre opened called the European Marine Energy Centre. Power cables were laid from the beach out into the sea, so any company with a new sea machine could plug it in and see if it survived real storms. Next to the island of Eday, in a stretch of water called the Fall of Warness, tidal turbines were dropped in, pulled out, broken, fixed, and dropped in again. Even when the money dried up, those cables stayed switched on.

The hardest part of making power from the sea is not building the machine. It is having a place where you are allowed to fail and try again.

Now for the problem these engineers were trying to fix. You have probably lived through it without realising. Think about a cold week in January. The weather goes calm and still. There is frost on the ground, and it gets dark before half past four. On days like this, the wind across Britain can almost stop. From the Dogger Bank out at sea to Whitelee near Glasgow, thousands of turbines slow right down. And that is exactly the week when everyone turns their heating up.

So the country switches on its gas power stations to fill the gap. But those gas stations have to be paid for and staffed all year round, just in case. A wind farm can tell you roughly how much power it will make tomorrow. It cannot tell you how much it will make next February.

Imagine owning a car that starts most mornings, but not every morning. You would still need a second car parked outside, just in case. And that second car costs money every day, whether you drive it or not. Those backup power stations are the second car. They sit there waiting for a few weeks a year, and every household in the country helps pay for them through their energy bill.

So imagine a type of power that does not depend on the weather at all. It is driven by the Moon. And to find it, we need to go to the very top of Britain.

Drive to John o' Groats, keep going a few miles east to the cliffs at Duncansby Head, and look out to sea. That is the Pentland Firth. On one side is the Atlantic Ocean. On the other side is the North Sea. The tide, which is the sea rising and falling, does not happen at the same time in both. The tide arrives at one end of the Firth hours before it reaches the other end. So the water level on one side is higher than the other, and the sea rushes through to even it out. Twice a day, a huge amount of ocean water pours through a gap only about seven miles wide. First one way, then back the other.

The water here moves faster than almost anywhere else around Britain. Sailing guides warn that in some places it can run at more than 15 knots, which is faster than most people can cycle. There is a whirlpool near the island of Stroma called the Swilkie. And off St John's Point, fishermen named a patch of rough water the Merry Men of Mey, where big waves break in open sea even on a calm day. Ships have been wrecked here for hundreds of years.

Between Stroma and the mainland is a narrower channel called the Inner Sound. Nobody lives on Stroma now. The last families left in 1962, and their stone houses have no roofs. But under the water next to the island, something is hard at work.

It is called MeyGen. And to understand why it works so well, you need one simple fact about water. Sea water is more than 800 times heavier than the same amount of air. So while a wind turbine needs a strong wind, a tidal turbine only needs water moving at walking speed, and that hits the blades as hard as a gale. A slow-moving tide carries the force of a storm.

Speed matters even more than weight. If the water moves twice as fast, the turbine does not make twice as much power. It makes eight times as much. The engineers did not choose this spot for the view. They chose it because it has some of the fastest water in Britain, and it is still shallow enough to build in.

But all that force caused a big problem. Fast water tries to rip things apart. So these turbines are built much tougher than the wind turbines you see on hills. Each one has three blades and measures 18 metres from tip to tip, close to the length of a cricket pitch. It sits on a heavy steel stand fixed to the seabed, in water deeper than a 10-storey block of flats is tall. Nothing sticks out of the sea, not even a floating marker. A ferry can sail right over the top.

The first turbines were put together at Nigg Energy Park on the Cromarty Firth, a yard that had built equipment for North Sea oil for years. In September 2016, the first turbine was shown to the public there. In November that year, it sent its first electricity to land through a cable laid along the seabed. To avoid digging up the cliffs, engineers drilled a tunnel underneath the shoreline and pulled the cable through it. By April 2018, four turbines were running in the Inner Sound, producing six megawatts together, enough to power thousands of homes, and the project began a planned working life of 25 years.

Putting them in place was a race against time. When the water is moving this fast, no diver can work and no crane can hold steady. So every lift had to happen in a short window called slack water. That is the brief moment when the tide is turning around and the sea goes calm. A large ship holds itself in position, lowers the turbine down on steel cables, and locks it onto its base before the water starts rushing again. If they miss that moment, the whole crew has to wait about six hours for the next one. Engineers here plan their week around the tide times, not the weather forecast.

Think about why that matters. For years, tidal power was only an experiment. One machine, tested for a few months, pulled out, and written up in a report. MeyGen was the first time anyone put several big tidal turbines next to each other and left them there to work like a real power station. The company behind it says it has now sent more electricity to the grid than any other group of tidal turbines in the world.

And MeyGen is not the only one. Remember Orkney, because this is where it all starts to connect.

Back at the Orkney test site, in April 2021, a company called Orbital Marine Power launched a machine built in Dundee called the O2. It is 74 metres long, with a bright yellow body that floats on top of the water. Two arms hang down into the sea, and each arm has a turbine at the end with blades about 20 metres across. When the tide changes direction, the blades turn around to face it. When it needs fixing, the arms lift up out of the water, so a small boat can reach it. No giant crane ship and no divers are needed. It is said to be the most powerful tidal turbine in the world. On Orkney, spare tidal power has even been used to make hydrogen, a clean fuel.

Further north, in Shetland, an Edinburgh company called Nova Innovation placed a group of smaller turbines on the seabed in 2016. They sit in Bluemull Sound, between the islands of Yell and Unst, and have been sending power to homes in Shetland ever since.

That is three machines with three different designs. One sits on the seabed. One floats on top of the water. One is small enough to be fixed from a fishing harbour. Britain did not put all its money on one design. It tested several at the same time in real sea water. That is exactly how Denmark became the world leader in wind power in the 1980s. Lots of small companies built different machines, and the weather weeded out the bad ones. Now the same thing is happening in Scottish waters.

But tests can only take you so far. At some point, the country had to decide if it really meant it.

It did. The government decides which new power projects get support through a kind of auction. For years, tidal power had to compete in that auction against offshore wind, which had a 10-year head start and huge factories behind it. Tidal could never win. Then, in 2022, the government set aside a part of the auction just for tidal power. MeyGen won a deal to add 28 more megawatts. Other tidal projects followed, from Orkney to a site off Holy Island in Anglesey, Wales. MeyGen also has permission to grow to almost 400 megawatts. The company says that at full size, it could power around 175,000 homes.

The guaranteed deals give companies a reason to build more turbines in the future. The test site in Orkney keeps people's skills sharp today. One does not work without the other.

Now for the honest part, because this story deserves it. Tidal power is still expensive. Right now, electricity from these early tidal projects costs several times more than electricity from offshore wind. But wind power was expensive once too, and its price dropped by about two thirds in ten years. That happened because thousands of wind turbines were built. Only a few dozen tidal turbines have been built so far.

The tide also stops for a while. Four times a day, when the tide is turning, the water goes calm for about an hour, and the turbines slow down and stop. Every two weeks, the tides are weaker, so they make less power. The power lines at the top of Scotland are also limited, and building new ones to carry power south is slow and expensive. Salt water also damages metal over time. Seals and porpoises hunt in these waters too, so cameras and underwater sound equipment watch the turbines day and night, and every new turbine must be proven safe for wildlife before it goes in. Pulling a broken turbine out of rough water to fix it costs far more than repairing a wind turbine on land. And the company behind MeyGen has been through money problems and a change of name. Sea power in Britain has let people down before.

Those risks are real. But this time, the sea itself is on Britain's side. And that brings us back to the number, and to the brass machine we saved for last.

In 1872, a Glasgow professor called William Thomson, who later became known as Lord Kelvin, designed a machine made of brass wheels, gears and one long wire. Each wheel represented one thing that pulls on the sea. One wheel for the Moon. One for the Sun. Others for the tilt of the Earth and the way the Moon's path changes over time. You turned a handle, and a pen drew the tides for a whole year ahead, for any harbour in the world. Working out a full year of tides took about four hours.

About 70 years later, machines like it helped win a war. In 1944, at the Liverpool Tidal Institute, a mathematician called Arthur Doodson was given a set of map points but no place names. He worked out the tides for a stretch of coast without being told it was Normandy. On the morning of D-Day, the landing boats came ashore on the tide his machines had predicted.

Here is why that matters for the Pentland Firth. The wind is unpredictable. Nobody can tell you if it will be windy next February. But the tide works like clockwork. It is controlled by the Moon and the Sun, and they have followed the same timetable for millions of years. So the engineers at MeyGen can do something no wind farm in the world can do.

They can tell you how much power their turbines will make at quarter past three on any Tuesday afternoon. Not just tomorrow. Not just next month. But for the whole working life of the turbines. That is the number. 25 years. Storms can change it a little. But the pattern of power for every single day is known before the turbine is even put in the water.

That is why energy experts around the world are paying attention. The most valuable thing about tidal power is not how much it makes. It is that you know exactly when it will make it.

Now put the pieces together. The Moon pulls the sea through the Firth. That pull creates the fastest water in Britain. Because water is so heavy, even a slow current carries huge force. The seabed hides the turbines from storms on the surface. The Orkney test site taught them how to survive. The government deals give companies a reason to build more. And because the tides in Pentland, Orkney, Shetland and Anglesey all peak at different times, a chain of tidal sites around the coast could hand power from one to the next, like runners passing a baton in a relay race.

Take away any one of those pieces, and the whole thing falls apart.

More than 150 years after a Glasgow professor built a brass machine that could predict the sea, the country that kept inventing sea power and selling it off may have finally found something it plans to keep. And right now it is spinning quietly in the dark, under the roughest water in Europe, where the tide has never once failed to come back.
